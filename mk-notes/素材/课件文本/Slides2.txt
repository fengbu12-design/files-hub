Recap: From Facts to Model
Vast disparities exist in per capita income across nations.
Two types of factors of production: capital and labor.
Hours worked per capita is roughly trendless, so increased labor
effort cannot explain long-run growth.
What drives growth then: capital accumulation or productivity
improvements?
Relatedly, are rich countries rich because they have more capital
than poorer countries, or because they are more productive?

===== SLIDE PAGE 3 =====
Solow Model
Solow model (Solow, 1953): used to study long-run growth and
cross-country income differences.
It does a nice job with stylized facts.
Main implications of model: productivity is key
Productivity key to sustained growth (not factor
accumulation)
Productivity key to understanding cross-country income
differences (not level of capital)
Model takes productivity to be exogenous. What is it? How to
increase it?

===== SLIDE PAGE 4 =====
Model Basics
Closed economy, with a unique final good.
Discrete time, indexed by t = 0, 1, 2, · · · .
Economy is inhabited by a large number of identical households,
whom we model as a single representative household.
Similarly, there are many firms, all using the same production
technology, so economy admits a representative firm.
Everything is denominated in units of the final good, which serves
as the numeraire (i.e., price normalized to 1 in each period).

===== SLIDE PAGE 5 =====
Households
Households will not be optimizing in the Solow model.
Each period, they simply save a constant, exogenous fraction
0 ≤s ≤1 of their disposable income, while consuming the
remaining 1 −s fraction.
Current saving becomes productive capital in the next period.
Households own the capital stock of the economy and rent it to
firms at rental price rt.
Households also own all the labor, which they supply inelastically
to firms, irrespective of market wage rate wt.

===== SLIDE PAGE 21 =====
Steady State Equilibrium
A steady-state equilibrium is an equilibrium in which kt = k∗for
all t.
The steady state is stable in the sense that for any initial k0̸ = 0,
kt will converge to k∗.
Once the economy arrives at the steady state, it sits there.
All other endogenous variables can be determined once we know
k∗.

===== SLIDE PAGE 29 =====
Interpreting the Dynamics of ↑A
When A ↑in period t, kt doesn’t change on impact since it is
predetermined, but it rises in future periods until reaching a permanently
higher level.
yt jumps up on impact as yt = Af (kt), and it further rises in future
periods both due to a higher A and rising kt.
ct and it dynamics similar to yt as each is a fraction of yt.
rt initially jumps up as rt = Af ′(kt), but will eventually fall back to initial
level at the new steady state (see algebraic example).
wt jumps up on impact as wt = AFL(Kt, L), and it further rises in future
periods both due to a higher A and rising Kt.

===== SLIDE PAGE 32 =====
Interpreting the Dynamics of ↑s
When s ↑in period t, kt doesn’t change on impact (predetermined), but
it rises in future periods until reaching a permanently higher level.
The same applies to yt since yt = Af (kt).
it jumps up immediately since it = syt, and it further rises in future
periods both due to a higher s and rising yt.
Since ct = (1 −s)yt, it must initially decline. Over time, since yt goes
up, whether ct ends up higher or lower than where it began is ambiguous,
though graph shows a higher steady state c∗.
rt gradually falls to a permanently lower level as kt gradually rises, given
rt = Af ′(kt) and f ′(kt) is decreasing in kt.
wt gradually rises to a permanently higher level given wt = AFL(Kt, L)
and Kt increases.

===== SLIDE PAGE 34 =====
Discussion
Neither changes in A nor s trigger sustained increases in
growth rates.
Each triggers faster growth temporarily while the economy
transitions to a new steady state.
In the long run, there is zero growth in this model—economy
converges to a steady state!
We will address that. However, model implies that sustained
growth must come from productivity increases:
A can increase indefinitely, while s is bounded by 1;
Repeated increases in s trigger continued declines in rt,
inconsistent with stylized facts.

Bottom Line
Sustained growth must come from productivity growth, not capital
accumulation.
You cannot save your way to more growth, because of diminishing
returns to capital.

Optimal Savings Rate
Household utility comes from consumption, not output.
Higher s has two opposing effects on consumption:
(i) More capital →more output →bigger “size of the pie” →
more consumption;
(ii) But get to consume a smaller “fraction of the pie” →less
consumption.

===== SLIDE PAGE 41 =====
Dynamic Inefficiency
Being “below” the Golden Rule does not necessarily imply the
economy is under-saving:
There is a dynamic tradeoff: ↑s today means less
consumption today, but more in the future
Whether this is desirable depends on how the future is valued
relative to today
But being “above” the golden rule cannot be optimal. This is
known as dynamic inefficiency:
By reducing s, could get more consumption both today and in
the future
Little or no evidence any modern economy is dynamically
inefficient.

Key Takeaways
We set out to study economic growth, but why write down a
model whose steady state has no growth?
The basic Solow model yields important insights about sources of
sustained economic growth.
Sustained growth must ultimately stem from productivity
improvements (technology), not just capital deepening.

Proof of the proposition follows from our assumption of constant
