===== SLIDE PAGE 2 =====
Introduction
New Keynesian models are the leading alternative to the
neoclassical / RBC model.
Demand side of NK models is identical to the neoclassical model.
“New” on the supply side: NK models assume nominal rigidities in
the short run, i.e., prices and nominal wages cannot respond to
shocks instantaneously.

===== SLIDE PAGE 3 =====
New Keynesian Implications
1. Money is non-neutral.
2. Demand shocks matter for output.
3. Equilibrium of the model is inefficient.
4. There is scope for policy to improve outcomes in the short run.

===== SLIDE PAGE 4 =====
Nominal Rigidities
Two basic variants of NK framework:
price stickiness
nominal wage stickiness
Each will require some change in the labor market – either the firm
(price stickiness) or the household (wage stickiness) operates off its
demand or supply schedule.
We focus on price stickiness as a motivating assumption.

===== SLIDE PAGE 5 =====
Demand Side
In the neoclassical model, classical dichotomy held: could
separately think about real and nominal demand.
Will no longer be true in NK models.
Instead use the aggregate demand (AD) curve to jointly summarize
real and nominal demand.
IS + LM = AD:
IS curve: same as before
LM curve: summarizes market for money

LM Curve
LM stands for “liquidity = money.”
Curve plots {rt, Yt} pairs for which money demand and Fisher
equation hold, taking Pt, πe

To derive the LM curve, convenient to draw money demand as a
function of rt instead of Pt, so the demand curve is
downward-sloping.

===== SLIDE PAGE 16 =====
Supply Side
Summarized by the AS curve, which stands for aggregate supply.
In general, AS curve plots combinations of (Pt, Yt) consistent with
(i) the production function, (ii) some notion of labor market
equilibrium, and (iii) any exogenous restriction on nominal prices or
wage adjustment.
We first look at AS curve in the neoclassical model, then show how
it can be derived in NK models.
Since Pt does not appear in neoclassical supply side, AS curve is
identical to Y s curve, except vertical axis is Pt instead of rt.

===== SLIDE PAGE 19 =====
New Keynesian Supply Side
NK supply side assumes nominal rigidities – prices or nominal
wages are completely or partially fixed in the short run.
Results in a non-vertical AS curve, thus allowing demand shocks
to have real effects on the economy.
We focus on price stickiness and consider two forms:
Simple sticky price model: price level is completely fixed
Partial sticky price model: price level is partially fixed

Simple Sticky Price Model
Pt = ¯Pt is now exogenous, rather than endogenous.
Replace labor demand curve with Pt = ¯Pt.
Firm has to hire labor to meet demand at ¯Pt rather than to
maximize its value.
Supply side conditions:
Nt
=
Ns(wt, θt)
Pt
=
¯Pt
Yt
=
AtF(Kt, Nt)
AS curve will just be horizontal at ¯Pt. Can only shift if ¯Pt changes
exogenously.

===== SLIDE PAGE 23 =====
Simple Sticky Price Model: Remarks
In this model, output is solely demand-determined:
Position of AD determines Yt
Given Yt, determine the required Nt
Given Nt, determine wt from labor supply curve
In a sense, this is opposite of neoclassical model:
Labor demand and supply jointly determine wt and Nt
Nt determines Yt
Position of AD determines Pt

===== SLIDE PAGE 26 =====
Monetary Non-Neutrality: Intuition
When Mt goes up, real interest rate falls, holding Pt fixed.
Sticky prices: households and firms increase spending in response
to lower real interest rate.
Flexible prices: Pt rises with Mt, leaving real interest rate
unchanged and no real effect.
Nominal rigidities are key: they prevent prices from fully absorbing
the money shock.
The stickier prices are, the bigger the real effect.

===== SLIDE PAGE 31 =====
Effects of Shocks: Simple Sticky Price vs. Neoclassical
Relative to neoclassical model, in the simple sticky price model:
Output over-reacts to demand shocks (nominal or real)
Output under-reacts to supply shocks
Basic intuition:
When demand goes up, if prices can’t rise, firms
accommodate demand by producing more
When productivity improves, if prices can’t fall, output is fixed
by demand, so firms need less labor

===== SLIDE PAGE 38 =====
Simple vs. Partial Sticky Price vs. Neoclassical
How the economy behaves in the partial sticky price model depends
on γ (the slope of AS curve, how sticky/flexible prices are).
Results from the partial sticky price model are in between the
extremes of the simple sticky price model (γ = 0) and the
neoclassical model (γ →∞).

Optimal Monetary Policy
We have looked at exogenous changes in monetary policy and
studied their real effects.
But optimal policy isn’t about randomly changing policy.
It is about endogenously reacting to other shocks.

===== SLIDE PAGE 40 =====
Dual Mandate and the Case for Price Stability
In the US, the Fed has a dual mandate: price stability and full
employment.
In model, think of dual mandate as:
Pt close to constant
Yt = Y f
t
In NK models, the “Divine Coincidence” (Blanchard and Gali,
2007) holds: stabilizing prices stabilizes output gap, and vice versa.

===== SLIDE PAGE 41 =====
Optimal Policy in a Nutshell
Assume economy initially in neoclassical, no output gap equilibrium.
Then some exogenous changes cause either the AD or AS curve to shift.
Will in general result in a non-zero output gap in the short run, which
will put pressure on ¯Pt to adjust in the medium run to shift the AS curve
to close the gap.
No need to wait for internal model dynamics to take over. Instead, adjust
policy (i.e., Mt) so that Yt = Y f
t .
Results in no change in price level. Both parts of the dual mandate are
satisfied.
Optimal policy involves:
Counteract demand shocks
Accommodate supply shocks
