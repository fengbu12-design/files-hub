Course in a Nutshell
Macroeconomics asks how the aggregate economy functions as a
whole.
Modern macroeconomics is not just observation; it is grounded in
models of dynamic and intertemporal decisions by individuals.
The central puzzle:
1. Long-run: Why are some nations rich and others poor?
2. Short-run: Why do booms turn to busts, and can policy fix
it?

Gross Domestic Product
GDP (nominal): current value of all final goods and services produced
within a country in a given time period.
Intermediate goods and services are excluded from the calculation.
E.g., the economy produced $240 worth of bread this year using $150
worth of flour as input, which was in turn produced by using $80 worth of
wheat as input. What is the GDP of the economy this year?

===== SLIDE PAGE 11 =====
Remark I
Left panel plots the natural log of real GDP for the US over
1947–2016.
Why natural logs? For small changes in x:
ln xt+1 −ln xt ≈(xt+1 −xt)/xt.
Strikingly, we can approximate log real GDP quite well with a
straight line, which we call a trend line.
As a first-order approximation, the US has grown at a constant
annual rate of roughly 3%, which is captured by the slope of the
trend line.

===== SLIDE PAGE 12 =====
Remark II
Right panel plots the detrended series, showing deviations of log
real GDP from the trend line.
Shaded areas indicate recession episodes, which are determined by
the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) as periods of
significant decline in economic activity that spread across the
economy and last for more than a few months.
The sharp decline in real GDP during recession periods is quite
visible.

===== SLIDE PAGE 13 =====
Economic Growth
When economists talk about “growth,” they typically mean the
average rate of growth in real GDP per capita over the long run.
Long run: frequencies of time measured in decades;
Not period-to-period fluctuation in the growth rate.
“Once you start to think about growth, it is difficult to think about
anything else.” —Robert Lucas, 1995 Nobel Prize winner.

Small differences in growth rates matter over long horizons.

Why These Facts Matter
We have established the stylized facts of economic growth. Now,
we must build a theory that explains them.
Understanding these patterns is the key to designing policies that
foster faster growth.
A good model must first match the data before we can trust its
predictions.
Next time, we will construct the Solow Model, our primary tool for
analyzing these dynamics.
