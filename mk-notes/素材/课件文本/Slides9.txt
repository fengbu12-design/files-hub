What is Money?
Three primary functions:
Medium of exchange: rather than engaging in barter, can
trade money for goods and services.
Store of value: a way to shift resources across time.
Unit of account: makes it easier to compare value across
different goods.

How to Incorporate Money in Neoclassical Model?
It is not easy.
Model has only one good, rendering the medium of exchange
function of money less interesting.
Household can store value via interest-bearing savings and firm via
physical capital.
Since money does not pay interest, how do we get people to want
to hold money in the model?
We’ll take a shortcut by assuming that household receives utility
from holding money.

Model Modifications
All constraints are now nominal—denominated in dollars, Pt.
Households derive utility from holding real money balances,
Mt/Pt, accrued in period t.
Savings pay nominal interest rate, it, distinct from the real rate, rt.
Government sets the money supply exogenously at Ms
t = Mt.
Key Takeaway: These modifications introduce two new
endogenous variables (Pt, it) requiring two new equilibrium
conditions, while leaving the original six real equations entirely
unchanged.

Firms
Since firm does not hold money, whether we write firm’s problem
in nominal or real terms does not affect the optimality conditions
and decision rules previously derived.
Firm borrowing, investment, and dividends are all expressed in
nominal terms.
In market clearing conditions, nominal prices appear on both sides
of each equation and therefore cancel out.
The equilibrium conditions for the real block of the model remain
identical to those derived previously.

===== SLIDE PAGE 14 =====
Classical Dichotomy
The first six equilibrium equations form the real block of the
model: hold independently of any reference to nominal variables.
Last two equations form the nominal block—a system of two
equations in two unknown endogenous variables {Pt, it}.
Do not need to know nominal variables to determine real
variables—known as the classical dichotomy.
Converse not true: nominal variables will be affected by real
variables.

===== SLIDE PAGE 17 =====
Monetary Neutrality
Changes in Mt do not affect equations in the real block—no effect
on any real endogenous variable.
Money is said to be neutral in the neoclassical model.
Useful medium-run benchmark, but in the short run nominal
rigidities may break monetary neutrality.
In this model, only effect of ↑Mt is to raise Pt (inflation).
What is the effect of ↑πe
t+1?

===== SLIDE PAGE 18 =====
Real Shocks Affect Nominal Variables
Positive supply shocks (↑At or ↓θt): lower rt and raise Yt, both
of which pivot money demand to the right, lowering Pt.
Positive demand shocks (↑At+1, ↑Gt, or ↓Gt+1): raise rt without
changing Yt. Hence, money demand pivots to the left, raising Pt.

Neoclassical Model and RBC Theory
Real business cycle (RBC) theory takes the neoclassical model not
just as an adequate description of an economy over the medium
run but as a good description of the economy in the short run.
Implications of RBC theory:
Money is neutral
Supply shocks (in particular, productivity shocks) drive
fluctuations
No role for active stabilization policies—equilibrium is efficient
Do we want to take these implications seriously?
Need to know whether model can fit the data.

Measuring Business Cycle
Business cycle refers to fluctuations in log real GDP and other
aggregate variables about some measure of trend.
A defining characteristic of long-run real GDP is that it trends up.
When moving away from long run, we’d like to remove the trend.

===== SLIDE PAGE 26 =====
Detrending
In principle, many ways to remove trend from the series.
One could fit a straight line through the actual series and take that
to be the trend.
Could also define trend as the average value of the actual series
over a specified time window.
In academic work, it is common to detrend using the
Hodrick-Prescott (HP) Filter.
It picks out the trend to minimize volatility of the cyclical
component, subject to a penalty for the trend itself moving around
too much.

===== SLIDE PAGE 27 =====
Taking Model to the Data
In the neoclassical model, output only reacts to supply shocks
(changes in At, θt).
Demand shocks don’t do anything to output; even in version of
model where Y s is non-vertical, they won’t do much.
1. In the data, cyclical components of Ct, Nt, etc. co-move with
Yt over the business cycle. Can model relying on exogenous
changes in At, θt reproduce these co-movements?
2. Is there any good evidence of changes in At or θt
corresponding to observed changes in Yt in the data?

===== SLIDE PAGE 30 =====
Policy Implications
Conditional on movements in At, model can produce movements
in endogenous variables which look like observed patterns of
aggregate series in data.
There is some evidence that At moves strongly with output,
lending empirical credence to the theory.
If it roughly fits the data, one is tempted to conclude that the
theory is good.
Policy implications: Recessions are efficient responses to
exogenously lower productivity; No justification for activist policies,
monetary or fiscal, to try to combat recessions.

===== SLIDE PAGE 31 =====
Do We Really Buy This?
Potential criticisms of RBC theory:
What exactly are these productivity shocks? Why don’t we
read about them in the newspaper?
To generate realistic movements in Yt, model needs to rely on
very elastic labor supply (i.e. labor supply curve very flat),
which seems at odds with micro data
Demand shocks don’t matter, money is neutral, markets are
frictionless. Do these seem right?
Is what we’re measuring as TFP really capturing exogenous
productivity in the model or something else?

Is TFP Appropriately Measured?
Suppose the true production function is:
Yt = At(utKt)αN1−α
t
ut: capital utilization. Can’t adjust Kt in short run, but can adjust
utilization.
But TFP as typically measured isn’t accounting for ut, so it’s
measuring not just At.
Demand shocks could be causing ut to move, making it look like
At is moving with Yt even if it isn’t.
Basu, Fernald, and Kimball (2006) construct a
“utilization-adjusted” measure of TFP and it is acyclical or even
countercyclical.

===== SLIDE PAGE 34 =====
Concluding Thoughts
Each of these criticisms (and others) has merit.
Today, few economists really believe that short-run fluctuations are
efficient responses to changes in productivity.
Neoclassical model is a useful benchmark, particularly for the
medium run.
But to think about short run business cycles and policy, need to
modify the framework to allow for demand shocks to matter,
money to be non-neutral, and equilibrium to be inefficient.
We do so next time by introducing the New Keynesian Model.
