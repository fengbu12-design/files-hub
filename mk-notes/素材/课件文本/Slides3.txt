Introduction
Solow model can reproduce time series stylized facts if it is
assumed that productivity grows over time.
Let’s now use the model to think about cross-country income
differences.
What explains these differences?

===== SLIDE PAGE 3 =====
Hypotheses
Three hypotheses for why cross-country income differences exist:
1. Countries are initially endowed with different levels of capital.
2. Countries have different savings rates.
3. Countries have different productivity levels.

===== SLIDE PAGE 4 =====
Convergence
Suppose two countries are otherwise identical, hence have the
same steady state.
Suppose country 2 is initially endowed with less capital: k2,t < k1,t,
and suppose country 1 is initially in steady state: k1,t = k∗.
Source: GLS Ch. 7.
3 / 14

===== SLIDE PAGE 5 =====
Catch-Up
If country 2 is initially endowed with less capital, it should grow
faster than country 1, eventually catching up with country 1, since
they have the same steady state.
Source: GLS Ch. 7.
4 / 14

===== SLIDE PAGE 6 =====
Is Convergence in the Data?
Correlation between initial GDP per capita in 1950 and cumulative
growth from 1950-2010 is weakly negative when focusing on all
countries. Too weak to support absolute convergence.
Source: GLS Ch. 7.

Focusing on a More Selective Group of Countries
Focusing only on OECD countries (more similar), story looks more
promising for convergence.

Pseudo Natural Experiment: WWII
WWII losers, Germany and Japan, grew faster for 20-30 years than
the winners, US and UK.
But don’t seem to catch up all the way to the US.
Conditional convergence: countries converge to different steady
states.

What Could It Be?
If countries have different steady states and differences in s cannot
plausibly account for this, must be differences in productivity.
Seems to be backed up in data: rich countries are highly

Take natural logs:
ln At = ln Yt −α ln Kt −(1 −α) ln Lt
We can measure Yt, Kt, Lt empirically, and if we are willing to
assume a value of α, we can recover ln At, which is called the
Solow residual, or (log) total factor productivity (TFP).
It is the part of output that cannot be explained by observable
capital and labor inputs.

===== SLIDE PAGE 13 =====
Productivity is King
Productivity is what drives everything in the Solow model.
Sustained growth must come from productivity.
Large income differences must come from productivity.
But what is productivity? Solow model doesn’t say.

Factors Influencing Productivity
Including but not limited to:

2. Climate
3. Geography
4. Institutions
5. Finance
6. Free trade
7. Infrastructure

===== SLIDE PAGE 15 =====
Policy Implications
If a country wants to become richer, needs to focus on policies
that promote productivity.
Example: Would giving computers (capital) to people in
sub-Saharan Africa help them get rich?
Not without the infrastructure to connect to the Internet, the
knowledge of how to use the computer, and the institutions to
protect property rights, etc.
