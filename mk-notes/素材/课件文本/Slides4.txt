===== SLIDE PAGE 4 =====
Setup of the Baseline Model
Representative household lives for two periods, t and t + 1.
Receives exogenous income in both periods, Yt, Yt+1 (future
income is known with certainty).
In period t, must decide how much to save/borrow and how much
to consume.
Savings (or borrowings), St, face a real interest rate rt, which the
household takes as given.
Do not model a financial intermediary (e.g., a bank), but assume
existence of option to borrow/save through this intermediary.

===== SLIDE PAGE 7 =====
Household Preferences
Lifetime utility is given by:
U = u(Ct) + βu(Ct+1).
0 < β < 1 is the discount factor.
Smaller β means less patience, i.e., discounting the future heavily.
u(·) maps consumption into utilities.
Utility is completely ordinal – no meaning to magnitude and can
be negative. Only useful for comparing alternatives.
Assume u′(·) > 0 and u′′(·) < 0.
More is better, but at a decreasing rate.

Euler Equation
To characterize optimal behavior, set ∂U/∂Ct = 0:
u′(Ct) = β(1 + rt)u′
(1 + rt)(Yt −Ct) + Yt+1

Yields the consumption Euler equation:
u′(Ct) = β(1 + rt)u′(Ct+1)
Interpretation: marginal cost of one unit of savings today (LHS)
must be equalized with marginal benefit of having more resources
for consumption tomorrow, viewed from today’s perspective (RHS).

===== SLIDE PAGE 20 =====
Interpreting Responses to ↑Yt
When Yt goes up, budget line shifts out, but slope remains
unchanged: −(1 + rt).
As a result, slope of IC at new optimum stays unchanged.
Must be that both Ct and Ct+1 go up, otherwise slope of IC at
new optimum would change.
For Ct+1 to go up, some of the increase in current income must be
saved to finance more consumption tomorrow.
Define ∂Ct
∂Yt as the marginal propensity to consume (MPC).
Result means (i) 0 < MPC < 1 and (ii) St must go up.

===== SLIDE PAGE 22 =====
Interpreting Responses to ↑Yt+1
As when Yt goes up, both Ct and Ct+1 will go up when Yt+1 goes up.
For Ct to go up, savings must decrease (or borrowing must increase).
Results show household wants to increase consumption in both periods
when income increases in either period.
That is, wants consumption to be smooth relative to income.
Due to diminishing marginal utility, it gives the household a higher utility
boost to increase consumption a bit in both periods than to increase
consumption by a lot only in one period – known as consumption
smoothing motive.
Achieves smoothing by adjusting saving behavior: increase St when Yt
goes up, reduce St when Yt+1 goes up.

===== SLIDE PAGE 23 =====
Exogenous Increase in rt
A little trickier.
Increase in rt makes budget line steeper, pivoting through the
endowment point.
Two effects:
Substitution effect: how would consumption bundle change if
income is adjusted for household to stay on original IC
Income effect: how would increase in rt make household
locate on a higher or lower IC
Substitution effect always reduces Ct, increases St.
Income effect depends on whether household is initially a borrower
(Ct > Yt) or saver (Ct < Yt).

===== SLIDE PAGE 29 =====
Application I: Permanent vs. Transitory Income Changes
We’ve analyzed how consumption bundle would react to changes in
either Yt or Yt+1.
In the data, however, income changes tend to be persistent, i.e.,
higher current income tends to be positively correlated with higher
future income.
For example, while a bonus would be a one-time change in income,
a pay raise increases current as well as future income.
Does consumption react the same way to transitory vs. permanent
income changes?

===== SLIDE PAGE 32 =====
Permanent Income Hypothesis (PIH)
Results arise naturally due to consumption smoothing motive.
Consistent with Friedman (1957) and the PIH:
Consumption is forward-looking
Ought to be a function of “permanent income”
MPC is less than one
Consumption would react more to events that change income
in a more persistent manner
Regressing Ct on Yt will not identify MPC if changes in Yt in
historical data are persistent (relevant for fiscal multiplier)

===== SLIDE PAGE 33 =====
Application II: Borrowing Constraint
In our baseline analysis, we’ve assumed household can freely
borrow or save.
In reality, many households face imperfect access to credit markets
and may not be able to borrow at all.
Suppose we impose St ≥0, which prohibits household borrowing in
period t.
When the borrowing constraint is binding, household would like to
choose Ct > Yt but cannot, so the best it can do is to consume
Ct = Yt.

===== SLIDE PAGE 37 =====
Policy Implications
With a binding borrowing constraint, MPC out of current income
becomes one and consumption ceases to be forward-looking.
Important policy implications regarding the effectiveness of tax
cuts.
Earlier, our analysis on transitory vs. permanent income changes
suggests that temporary tax cuts would have smaller effect than
permanent tax cuts.
But if part of the population is borrowing constrained, targeted
and temporary tax cuts may nonetheless stimulate consumption,
which is attractive considering that permanent tax cuts are often
politically unpopular due to their effect on national debt.
