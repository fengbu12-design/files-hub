Introduction
The term fiscal policy refers to government spending and
taxes/transfers.
We will study fiscal policy in a particularly simple environment –
endowment economy.
Basic conclusions carry over to a model with production.
Key result: Ricardian equivalence, which says how a government
finances its spending (debt or taxes) is irrelevant for understanding
the equilibrium effect of a change in spending.
Not a general result; only holds under special circumstances.
But useful benchmark for intuition.
We will also discuss the “government spending multiplier.”

Taxes Drop Out
Both Tt and Tt+1 drop out.
From household’s perspective, it is as though Tt = Gt and
Tt+1 = Gt+1.
This means the consumption function can be rewritten as:

===== SLIDE PAGE 10 =====
Intuition
All the household cares about when making consumption/saving
decision is the present discounted value of stream of net income.
A tax cut, not met by a cut in government spending, means future
taxes must go up by an equal amount in present value, which is
internalized by the household.
Example:
Cut Tt by 1
Holding Gt and Gt+1 fixed, government IBC requires Tt+1 to
go up by (1 + rt)
Present value of future tax increase is 1, the same as the
present value of current tax cut – it’s a wash from the
household’s perspective

===== SLIDE PAGE 11 =====
Ricardian Equivalence
Conceived by David Ricardo and formalized by Barro (1974).
Basic insight: the manner of government finance is irrelevant for
how a change in government spending impacts the economy.
Increasing Gt by increasing Tt (“tax finance”) will have equivalent
effects to increasing Gt by increasing Bt (“deficit finance”).
Why? Current debt is equivalent to future taxes, and household is
forward-looking.

===== SLIDE PAGE 12 =====
Ricardian Equivalence in the Real World
Ricardian equivalence rests on several dubious assumptions:
Taxes must be lump sum (non-distortionary)
No borrowing constraint
Household forward-looking
Government does not “outlive” households (so timing of tax
collection does not matter)
Nevertheless, basic intuition of Ricardian equivalence is potentially
powerful when thinking about the real world.

Market clearing requires “aggregate saving” to be zero:

Equivalently:
Yt −Tt −Ct −(Gt −Tt) = 0,
which implies:
Yt = Ct + Gt

Equilibrium Conditions
Household optimization:
Ct = C d(Yt −Gt, Yt+1 −Gt+1, rt)
Market clearing:
Yt = Ct + Gt
Exogenous variables: Yt, Yt+1, Gt, Gt+1 (do not need to know
debt or taxes – this is the implication of Ricardian Equivalence).
System of equations will determine endogenous Ct and rt (and
hence Ct+1 from household IBC).
IS and Y s curves are conceptually the same as before, but now Gt
and Gt+1 will shift the IS curve.

“Rounds of Spending” Intuition
One can think of multiplier as resulting from several rounds of
spending happening within a period.
In round 1, government spending goes up by 1.
Without Ricardian equivalence, this generates 1 extra income,
which generates MPC of extra consumption in round 2. This extra
MPC of consumption in round 2 generates MPC extra income,
which generates MPC 2 extra consumption in round 3, and so on:

dGt
= 1 + MPC + MPC 2 + MPC 3 + · · · =
1
1 −MPC
With Ricardian equivalence, process is similar, but initially only

===== SLIDE PAGE 20 =====
Crowding Out
An increase in Gt has no effect on Yt in equilibrium.
Hence, private consumption is completely “crowded out,” i.e.,
dCt = −dGt.
To make this compatible with market clearing, rt must rise.
Increase in Gt+1 has opposite effect: rt falls to keep Ct from
declining.
Again, rt adjusts so as to undo any desired smoothing behavior by
household.
Multiplier is zero in equilibrium. Not a consequence of Ricardian
equivalence, but rather due to assumption of endowment economy
where output cannot respond.
